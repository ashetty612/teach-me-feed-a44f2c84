Case Rounds · Ophtho Grand Rounds: The Painless Vision Loss Differential (CRAO, NAION, GCA, retinal detachment)

Download MP3 · Plain-text transcript

Key points

  • Painless monocular vision loss is never elective and requires immediate evaluation, confirming monocularity, checking for RAPD, and examining the fundus.
  • Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO) is an acute ischemic stroke that requires immediate transfer to an emergency department for a comprehensive stroke evaluation.
  • Non-Arteritic Anterior Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (NAION) is diagnosed after ruling out Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA) and is characterized by disc edema, often an altitudinal field defect, and a 'disc at risk' in the fellow eye; optic nerve sheath decompression offers no benefit.
  • Suspected Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA) necessitates immediate high-dose glucocorticoid treatment, even before temporal artery biopsy, to protect the fellow eye from vision loss.
  • Approximately 4% of biopsy-proven GCA cases can present with both normal erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, meaning normal labs do not rule out the diagnosis.
  • Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD), especially macula-on cases or those involving superior bullous detachments near the arcade, is a surgical emergency requiring same-day referral to retina, regardless of initial preserved acuity.
  • Conservative maneuvers for CRAO (e.g., ocular massage, anterior chamber paracentesis) lack compelling evidence of benefit and should not delay emergency department transfer.

Pearls

  • Painless is not elective. Confirm it is monocular, check for an RAPD, and look at disc, inner retina, and vitreous before you assign a clock.
  • Eye stroke uses the same door as brain stroke, the emergency department, not tomorrow's opening.
  • Crowded fellow disc, altitudinal field, morning discovery, after you have ruled out giant cell arteritis, is NAION, and the sheath does not get opened.
  • Steroids first, biopsy second. The biopsy justifies the taper, not the first dose; treat the second eye tonight.
  • Flashes, floaters, curtain—the macula tells you how many hours you have, not whether you have a problem.

Self-test

  1. A man has sudden painless count-fingers vision, a dense RAPD, a cherry-red spot, and a normal disc. What door does he use, and why?
    AnswerThe emergency department, because CRAO with retinal infarction is a form of acute ischemic stroke, and clinic is not the place to run that pathway.
  2. Why start high-dose steroids for suspected giant cell arteritis before the temporal artery biopsy comes back?
    AnswerSteroids protect the fellow eye now. The biopsy confirms the diagnosis and justifies the taper. About four percent of biopsy-proven disease has normal inflammatory markers, so the labs are not a veto.
  3. Visual acuity is twenty twenty-five, there is a superior curtain, and optical coherence tomography shows an attached fovea. What do you tell the patient about timing?
    AnswerThis is a same-day call to retina. The sources suggest a possible advantage to repairing macula-on detachments within twenty-four hours, and they do not give you permission to wait because the acuity still looks good.

Chapter 2: Case 1, C R A O

Maya: We dilate. The right arteries are attenuated. There is boxcarring of the blood column, whitening of the posterior pole, and a cherry-red spot at the fovea. The disc is not swollen. The left fundus is normal.

Theo: Inner retina is opaque, fovea is still red, disc is quiet. That is central retinal artery occlusion, not a detached retina and not disc edema.

Chapter 3: Case 2, nay-on

Maya: New patient. Sixty-two, hypertension, diabetes, untreated sleep apnea. He noticed a gray shade over the lower half of the left vision when he woke up. It has been six hours and it is not changing.

Theo: Waking up with an altitudinal shade makes me think optic nerve more than artery or detachment, but I want the exam.

Maya: Visual acuity is twenty sixty. There is a left R A P D. The left disc is swollen and slightly hyperemic, more superiorly, with a few flame hemorrhages. The right disc is small and crowded, almost no cup. The field is a dense inferior altitudinal defect.

Theo: Like a swollen ankle in a tight ski boot.

Chapter 4: Case 3, giant cell arteritis

Maya: Seventy-six-year-old woman. She lost the right vision this morning. For three weeks, a new temporal headache, pain when she chews a bagel, and scalp tenderness under the hairbrush. Acuity is hand motion. Dense R A P D. The right disc is swollen and chalky pale. The left disc has a healthy cup.

Chapter 5: Case 4, the curtain

Maya: Last case. Fifty-five, eight diopters of myopia. Flashes in the left eye two nights ago, a shower of new floaters yesterday, and this morning a dark curtain rising from below. Visual acuity is still twenty twenty-five. No real R A P D.

Theo: Preserved acuity and a weak pupil argue against a dense inner-retinal or optic-nerve infarct. The curtain makes me think detached retina, still sparing the fovea.

Maya: Anterior vitreous shows tobacco dust, Shafer's sign. There is a horseshoe tear at eleven o'clock and a bullous superior rhegmatogenous detachment whose posterior edge sits just outside the arcade. Optical coherence tomography confirms the fovea is attached.

Theo: Macula-on superior bullous detachment, new tear, myope. This is the case people underestimate because they can still read the chart.

Maya: Mechanism once. A posterior vitreous detachment tugs on lattice and the vitreous base, opens a full-thickness tear, and lets liquefied vitreous under the retina. Photopsias are traction. New floaters may be collagen, a Weiss ring, or blood. The curtain is detached retina.

Theo: Pause and predict. Twenty twenty-five with a curtain: how many hours do we have? Pause here.

Maya: The sources do not settle a rigid cutoff, and you should say that out loud. Reviews of moderate- to low-quality evidence suggest that repairing a macula-on detachment within zero to twenty-four hours of presentation may yield better final acuity than waiting longer.

Theo: And if the macula is already off?

Maya: Those same reviews suggest repair within zero to three days of symptom onset may be better than waiting four to seven days. Superotemporal bullous macula-on cases, and any detachment already at the arcade, are treated as the most urgent because they can peel through the fovea overnight.

Theo: Incidence, so I have a scale.

Maya: About ten to eighteen per one hundred thousand people per year for rhegmatogenous detachment. A preferred practice pattern reports that five point four to eight percent of patients with acute posterior vitreous detachment symptoms have a tear at the first exam.

Maya: The sources do not settle one exact tear rate, so use the clinical rule: new flashes and floaters get a same-day dilated exam with depression, not a return-if-worse handout.

Theo: Push-back time. If she can still see twenty twenty-five, why can't retina see her in a couple of days? Operating rooms are not infinite.

Maya: Because twenty twenty-five means the fovea is still attached, not that the problem is small. Once the fovea peels, you are arguing about how much acuity you can get back, not whether you can keep what she has.

Theo: So I position her with the detached retina dependent, call retina the same day, and talk through pneumatic retinopexy versus vitrectomy or a buckle depending on the tear.

Maya: Yes. Position and a same-day call are the things you control in clinic while a team is assembled.

Theo: Pearl: flashes, floaters, curtain, and the macula tells you how many hours you have, not whether you have a problem.

Maya: Memory hook: twenty twenty with a curtain is more urgent than twenty two hundred without one.
