Deep Dive · Ophtho Grand Rounds: The Painless Vision Loss Differential (CRAO, NAION, GCA, retinal detachment)

Download MP3 · Plain-text transcript

Key points

  • Painless monocular vision loss is a surgical, stroke, or steroid emergency; "painless does not mean elective."
  • Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO) is an eye stroke, diagnosed by inner-retinal whitening and cherry-red spot, requiring immediate ED transfer for stroke workup.
  • Experimental retinal tolerance to CRAO ischemia is 97 to 240 minutes, with only 17.7% functional recovery untreated.
  • Non-arteritic Anterior Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (NAION) involves sectoral disc edema in a "disc at risk," with IONDT proving optic nerve sheath decompression ineffective; fellow eye risk is 14.7% over ~5 years.
  • Arteritic AION from Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA) presents with chalky-white disc swelling and systemic symptoms; immediate high-dose steroids are critical, as ~4% of biopsy-proven cases have normal ESR/CRP.
  • Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment (RRD) involves flashes, floaters, and a "curtain"; macula-on RRD is a same-day surgical emergency.
  • The initial 60-second sort (RAPD, fundus exam) dictates the entire clinical outcome, routing patients to stroke, steroid, or surgical pathways.
  • Equating the time of discovery with the time of onset is a major diagnostic pitfall, as patients are notoriously poor at recognizing monocular visual deficits.

Pearls

  • Eye stroke uses the same door as brain stroke — the emergency department, not tomorrow’s opening.
  • Crowded fellow disc plus altitudinal field plus morning discovery, after you have ruled out GCA, is NAION — and the sheath does not get opened.
  • Steroids first, biopsy second. The biopsy justifies the taper, not the first dose.
  • Flashes, floaters, curtain — and the macula tells you how many hours you have, not whether you have a problem.
  • The four cases are one skill: matching anatomy to a clock.
