Pearls in 10 · How mRNA Vaccines Work

Download MP3 · Plain-text transcript

Key points

  • mRNA delivers temporary genetic instructions to cells, which then produce antigens for immune training.
  • Manufacturing uses cell-free in vitro transcription (IVT) for rapid design and production, allowing quick adaptation by only swapping the sequence.
  • The mRNA cannot alter cellular DNA as it remains in the cytoplasm, is not reverse-transcribed, and degrades within days.
  • Modified nucleosides (e.g., N1-methylpseudouridine) prevent innate immune detection of foreign RNA and improve its translation efficiency.
  • Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) encapsulate the mRNA, delivering it into cells where an ionizable lipid facilitates mRNA escape from acidic endosomes.
  • Antigen design, such as two proline substitutions ('2P') in the spike protein, stabilizes the target in its pre-fusion shape for optimal antibody recognition.
  • mRNA vaccines stimulate both antibody production (via MHC class II and CD4+ T cells) and CD8+ killer T-cell responses (via MHC class I).
  • Initial trials demonstrated approximately 95% relative risk reduction against symptomatic COVID-19, though protection against infection waned over time and with new variants.

Pearls

  • mRNA vaccines do not alter your DNA; the mRNA remains in the cytoplasm, is not reverse-transcribed, and degrades within days.
  • "95% efficacy" refers to a 95% relative risk reduction in symptomatic cases compared to a placebo group, not that 5% of vaccinated individuals will get sick.
  • Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) not only deliver mRNA but also act as an adjuvant, contributing to common vaccine side effects like sore arm and fever.
  • Protection against severe disease from mRNA vaccines generally holds up better than protection against infection, especially with new variants, necessitating updated boosters.
  • A rare but notable risk of mRNA vaccination is myocarditis, particularly in young males after the second dose.

Self-test

  1. Why do mRNA vaccines prime CD8 killer T cells when many protein vaccines don't?
    AnswerThe protein is made inside your cells, so fragments get displayed on MHC class one molecules, which trains CD8 killer T cells.
  2. What does "ninety-five percent efficacy" actually mean?
    AnswerIt means a ninety-five percent relative reduction in symptomatic cases versus a placebo group during the trial window.
  3. Why is the lipid ionizable instead of permanently charged?
    AnswerIt allows the lipid to be positive in acidic conditions (for packing RNA and escaping the endosome) but near-neutral at the body's neutral pH for reduced toxicity.

Cold open

Theo: Maya, January eleventh, 2020. A string of letters gets posted online. A, U, G, C, about thirty thousand of them.

Maya: The genetic sequence of a new coronavirus. And here's the part that still gets me: within about sixty-six days, a vaccine built from that text file was going into the arm of the first volunteer in a Phase 1 trial.

Theo: Sixty-six days. Normal vaccines take years. So today's question is how. How do you turn a text file into a vaccine?

Maya: Three chapters. First, the message itself, what mRNA is. Second, the engineering tricks that made it actually work in a human body. Third, what the immune system does with it, what the evidence shows, and where it's heading.

Theo: And I'm going to push back every time you hand-wave.

Maya: I'd expect nothing less.

Chapter 1: The message

Maya: Start with the central dogma. DNA sits in the nucleus. When a cell wants a protein, it copies the relevant gene into messenger RNA, mRNA. That copy leaves the nucleus, finds a ribosome in the cytoplasm, and the ribosome reads it three letters at a time to build the protein.

Theo: So DNA is the master cookbook locked in the library, and mRNA is a photocopied recipe card.

Maya: A recipe card printed on paper that dissolves within days. That fading is a feature. Now, a mature mRNA has five parts. A five-prime cap at the front, which tells the ribosome "start here" and protects the end. A five-prime untranslated region. The coding sequence, which is the actual recipe. A three-prime untranslated region. And a poly-A tail, a long run of A's that works like a fuse: as it shortens, the message gets degraded.

Theo: Cap, five-prime UTR, coding sequence, three-prime UTR, tail. Got it. And the vaccine makers just, what, grow this in cells?

Maya: No, and that's the speed secret. They make it in a test tube. It's called in vitro transcription. You take a DNA template, add an enzyme called T7 RNA polymerase and the nucleotide building blocks, and it churns out RNA. Then you cap it and purify it. No cells, no eggs, no bioreactors full of virus.

Theo: So changing the vaccine means changing the template.

Maya: Exactly. Swap the sequence, same factory. That's how Moderna went from a posted genome to a trial dose by March sixteenth, 2020.

Theo: Okay, here's my push. The thing everybody worried about. Does this change my DNA?

Maya: No. And not for a vague reassuring reason. The mRNA stays in the cytoplasm. It never enters the nucleus, where DNA lives. Ordinary cells have no machinery to reverse-transcribe a message like this into DNA and stitch it in. And the molecule is broken down within days.

Theo: So the recipe card never goes back into the library.

Maya: Right. And where the analogy breaks: a real recipe card can be photocopied again. This one can't copy itself at all. Recap: mRNA is a short-lived instruction read in the cytoplasm, it's made cell-free so it's fast to redesign, and it can't rewrite your genome.

Chapter 2: The engineering

Theo: So if it's that simple, why didn't we have mRNA vaccines in the nineties?

Maya: Because naked RNA injected into a body is a disaster. Your immune system treats foreign RNA as a sign of viral infection. Sensors like Toll-like receptors three, seven and eight, and a cytoplasmic sensor called RIG-I, light up, inflammation spikes, and the RNA gets shredded before it makes much protein.

Theo: So the cell sees the recipe card and assumes it's a forged note from a burglar.

Maya: Good way to put it. The fix came from Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman. In a 2005 paper in the journal Immunity, they showed that if you swap one of RNA's letters, uridine, for a slightly modified version called pseudouridine, the sensors largely stop recognizing it. Later work showed modified RNA is also translated more efficiently.

Theo: And that earned them the Nobel.

Maya: The 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Today both licensed COVID vaccines use a cousin of that, N1-methylpseudouridine.

Theo: Fix number one: disguise. What's fix number two?

Maya: Design the message well. Many amino acids have multiple codons, so you can choose spellings that translate better and are more stable. And for the coronavirus they did something clever with the protein itself. The spike protein changes shape when it fuses with a cell. Researchers added two proline substitutions, called "two-P", that lock spike in its pre-fusion shape, the shape antibodies need to recognize to block the virus.

Theo: So you're showing the immune system a mugshot of the burglar before he breaks in, not after.

Maya: Ha, yes. Fix number three is the delivery truck: lipid nanoparticles. The RNA is wrapped in tiny fat bubbles, around eighty to a hundred nanometers, made of four lipids. An ionizable lipid, a phospholipid, cholesterol, and a PEG-lipid.

Theo: Why "ionizable" and not just positively charged? RNA is negative, so a positive lipid would grab it.

Maya: That's exactly the trick. The ionizable lipid is positive in acidic conditions, which is how it grabs the RNA during manufacturing. At the body's neutral pH it's close to uncharged, which makes it far less toxic. Then a cell swallows the particle into a compartment called an endosome, the endosome acidifies, the lipid becomes positive again, disrupts the membrane, and the RNA escapes into the cytoplasm.

Theo: A self-opening envelope that only unseals in the acidic mail room.

Maya: And honest caveat: only a small fraction of the RNA actually escapes the endosome. Estimates vary between studies, so the sources don't settle an exact number.

Theo: And the lipids are just inert packaging?

Maya: That's the misconception. The nanoparticle itself is inflammatory. It acts partly as an adjuvant, and it's a big reason for the sore arm and the day of fever. Recap: modified nucleosides hide the RNA from alarm sensors, smart sequence design plus two-P gives a better antigen, and lipid nanoparticles deliver it and help kick the immune system awake.

Chapter 3: The immune response, evidence, and frontier

Theo: Okay, the RNA is in the cell, the ribosome builds spike. Then what?

Maya: Here's where mRNA has an edge over a plain protein shot. Because the protein is made inside your cells, fragments get displayed on MHC class one molecules. That trains CD8 killer T cells. Spike that's released or picked up by immune cells gets shown on MHC class two, which activates CD4 helper T cells, and those helpers drive B cells in germinal centers to produce better and better antibodies.

Theo: So you get both arms. Antibodies and killer T cells.

Maya: Both arms. Now the evidence. Pfizer-BioNTech's trial, published by Polack and colleagues in the New England Journal in December 2020, enrolled about forty-three thousand people and found ninety-five percent efficacy against symptomatic COVID-19. Moderna's trial, Baden and colleagues, early 2021, about thirty thousand people, ninety-four point one percent.

Theo: Push-back time. Ninety-five percent means five percent of vaccinated people got sick?

Maya: No, and this is the most misunderstood number of the pandemic. It's a relative risk reduction. In the trial window, vaccinated people had about ninety-five percent fewer symptomatic cases than the placebo group. The absolute number of cases in both groups was small.

Theo: And then Omicron happened.

Maya: Right. Protection against infection waned over months and dropped with new variants, while protection against severe disease held up better. That's the reason for updated boosters.

Theo: Safety?

Maya: Common side effects are a sore arm, fatigue, and fever for a day or two. The notable rare risk is myocarditis, inflammation of the heart muscle, highest in young males after the second dose, as described by Oster and colleagues in JAMA in 2022.

Theo: Last stop, the frontier. Where does this go next?

Maya: Self-amplifying RNA, which copies itself inside the cell so you can use a smaller dose. Combination flu and COVID shots. And the one I find most exciting: personalized cancer vaccines. Moderna and Merck's mRNA-4157, paired with the immunotherapy pembrolizumab, reduced recurrence or death in a Phase 2b melanoma trial published in the Lancet in 2024. Each patient's vaccine encodes mutations from their own tumor.

Theo: Which loops right back to chapter one. It's just a text file. Change the text, change the vaccine.

Maya: That's the whole platform in one sentence.

Three: modified nucleosides stop the immune system from destroying it. Four: lipid nanoparticles deliver it, open in the acidic endosome, and add adjuvant punch. Five: it trains antibodies and T cells, with roughly ninety-five percent efficacy in the original trials and waning against variants.
